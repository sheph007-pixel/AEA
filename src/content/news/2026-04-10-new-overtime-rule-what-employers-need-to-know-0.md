## Overview of the Overtime Rule Changes

The U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) has recently announced updates to the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) regarding overtime pay. While the DOL has proposed changes that may take effect on July 1, 2026, it is important to note that this date is contingent upon the final approval of the proposed rule and could be subject to further delays. Employers, particularly those in the small to mid-sized business sector, should stay informed and prepared to adjust their payroll practices accordingly, as the proposed changes could be delayed or altered significantly.

## Key Changes to Salary Thresholds

Under the proposed regulations, the salary threshold for exempt employees may increase from the current level of $684 per week, which is based on the last update in 2020. This current threshold may not reflect future adjustments or proposals that could occur before the changes are finalized. The DOL has indicated that the new threshold could be set at $1,059 per week; however, this figure is speculative and not confirmed. Employers should not rely on this number as definitive, as it may change before implementation. This potential increase aims to ensure that more employees receive overtime pay, thereby promoting fair compensation and addressing wage stagnation issues.

Additionally, the rule may introduce a mechanism for automatic updates to the salary threshold every three years, but this is not guaranteed and should be viewed as a possibility rather than a certainty. Employers should anticipate ongoing adjustments in the future. This change aims to keep pace with inflation and changes in the cost of living, making it essential for HR professionals and business owners to regularly review their payroll systems.

## What Employers Need to Know

Employers should begin evaluating their current employee classifications and compensation structures to identify who may be affected by these proposed changes. With the potential increase in the salary threshold, many employees who were previously classified as exempt may need to be reclassified as non-exempt, making them eligible for overtime pay.

To prepare for this transition, employers should consider the following action items:

1. **Review Employee Classifications:** Audit current employee roles and their respective salaries to determine if any employees fall below the proposed new threshold.
  
2. **Evaluate Compensation Strategies:** Assess whether to adjust salaries to maintain exempt status or to revise pay structures for non-exempt employees to comply with potential new overtime requirements.

3. **Communicate Changes:** Keep employees informed about any changes to their classifications or compensation. Transparent communication can help mitigate confusion and ensure a smoother transition.

4. **Consult Legal Counsel:** Given the complexity of employment law and potential legal implications, it may be prudent to consult with legal or HR experts to ensure compliance with any new regulations.

## Understanding the Broader Implications

The DOL's proposed updated overtime rule aligns with a broader movement toward increased worker protections and fair labor practices. As more employees may become eligible for overtime, businesses could experience shifts in labor costs and employee morale. Employers must be proactive in adapting to these changes, not only to comply with the law but also to foster a positive workplace culture.

As the effective date of the proposed regulations approaches, HR professionals and business owners should prioritize these updates in their planning. With careful preparation and strategic adjustments, companies can navigate this regulatory change effectively, ensuring compliance while maintaining a motivated and fairly compensated workforce.
