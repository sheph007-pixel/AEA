## The Privacy Landscape Is Shifting

A growing number of states are extending comprehensive privacy protections to cover employee data, reversing a common exemption that previously shielded employment-related information from state privacy laws. In 2026 alone, new or expanded privacy laws in Texas, Florida, Indiana, and Tennessee have taken effect, joining existing frameworks in California, Colorado, Connecticut, and Virginia.

For employers, particularly those operating across multiple states, this means new obligations around how employee personal data is collected, stored, used, and shared.

## What Is Changing

### Employee Data Is No Longer Exempt
Many early state privacy laws, including those in Virginia and Colorado, initially exempted data collected in an employment context. Several states have now removed or narrowed these exemptions, meaning that employee personal information — from Social Security numbers and health data to biometric information and location tracking — is subject to the same protections as consumer data.

### Key Requirements Employers May Face
- **Notice at collection**: Employers may need to inform employees about what personal data is collected, why it is collected, and how it will be used — before or at the time of collection
- **Purpose limitation**: Employee data may only be used for the purposes disclosed at the time of collection, or for purposes the employee would reasonably expect
- **Data minimization**: Employers should collect only the minimum amount of personal data necessary for the stated purpose
- **Right to access and delete**: Employees may have the right to request access to their personal data and, in some cases, request its deletion
- **Opt-out of sale or sharing**: Where employee data is shared with third parties for purposes beyond employment administration, employees may have the right to opt out

### Biometric Data Gets Special Attention
Several states have enacted or strengthened biometric privacy laws that apply to the workplace. Employers using fingerprint scanners, facial recognition for time tracking, or other biometric systems should verify that they have obtained appropriate consent and are complying with applicable retention and destruction requirements.

## What Employers Should Do

- **Map your employee data**: Conduct a data inventory to identify what employee personal data you collect, where it is stored, who has access, and how long it is retained
- **Update privacy notices**: Draft or revise employee privacy notices to meet the disclosure requirements in each applicable state
- **Review vendor agreements**: Ensure that third-party vendors processing employee data (payroll providers, benefits administrators, background check services) have adequate data protection provisions in their contracts
- **Implement data access request procedures**: Establish a process for employees to submit and receive responses to data access, correction, and deletion requests
- **Train HR and IT teams**: Ensure that staff responsible for handling employee data understand the new requirements and can respond to employee requests within statutory deadlines
- **Consult legal counsel**: Given the variation across states, employers should work with privacy counsel to develop a compliance strategy that accounts for all applicable jurisdictions
