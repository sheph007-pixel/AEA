## Revised Federal Guidelines for Background Checks

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) jointly released updated guidelines on May 15, 2026, regarding the use of background checks in hiring processes. These guidelines aim to ensure compliance with the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) and Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Employers must now consider these changes when conducting background checks to avoid legal pitfalls.

## Key Changes and Compliance Requirements

The revised guidelines emphasize the necessity for employers to obtain explicit consent from job applicants before conducting background checks. Under the FCRA (15 U.S.C. § 1681b), employers must provide a clear and conspicuous disclosure, separate from other documents, informing candidates of the background check. This disclosure must be signed by the applicant, granting permission to proceed.

Additionally, the EEOC's guidance highlights the importance of avoiding discriminatory practices. Employers must ensure that their use of background checks does not result in disparate impact discrimination. This means that any policies or practices that disproportionately affect a protected group must be job-related and consistent with business necessity.

## Action Items for Employers

Employers should take immediate steps to align their hiring practices with these updated guidelines. First, review and update disclosure and authorization forms to ensure compliance with the FCRA requirements. Make sure these forms are separate documents and not embedded within employment applications.

Second, audit current background check policies to identify any potential disparate impact issues. Employers should evaluate whether their criteria for disqualifying candidates based on background checks are directly related to job performance and necessary for the business.

Lastly, train HR personnel and hiring managers on the new guidelines to ensure consistent and fair application across all candidates. This training should cover both legal requirements and best practices for conducting background checks.

## Implications for Non-Compliance

Failure to comply with these guidelines can lead to significant legal and financial consequences. Non-compliance with the FCRA can result in statutory damages ranging from $100 to $1,000 per violation, as well as potential punitive damages. Additionally, violations of Title VII can lead to lawsuits and reputational damage.

By taking proactive measures to update and enforce compliant background check procedures, employers can mitigate these risks and ensure a fair hiring process.
