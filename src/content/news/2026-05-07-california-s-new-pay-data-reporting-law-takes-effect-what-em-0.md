## California's SB 1162 Expands Pay Data Reporting Requirements

California's Senate Bill 1162, which significantly expands pay data reporting requirements for employers, takes effect on May 10, 2026. The bill amends the existing California Government Code § 12999, requiring private employers with 100 or more employees to submit detailed pay data reports to the state's Department of Fair Employment and Housing (DFEH).

## Expanded Reporting Obligations

Under SB 1162, employers must now include median and mean hourly rates within each job category, broken down by race, ethnicity, and sex. This goes beyond the previously required data, which only included the number of employees in each category. The new law also mandates that employers disclose pay data for remote workers based in California, regardless of where the employer is headquartered.

## Compliance Action Items

Employers must prepare to submit their first expanded report by May 31, 2027. To comply, HR departments should immediately begin auditing their current pay practices to ensure accurate data collection. This includes verifying that all employee demographic information is up-to-date and categorized correctly.

Additionally, businesses should review their compensation structures to identify any potential disparities that could attract regulatory scrutiny. Employers may need to implement new payroll systems or adjust existing ones to streamline the collection and reporting of required data.

## Potential Penalties

Non-compliance with SB 1162 could result in significant penalties. The DFEH has the authority to seek court orders requiring employers to comply with the reporting requirements. Furthermore, employers may face fines of up to $100 per employee for initial violations and $200 per employee for subsequent violations.

## Legal Considerations

Employers should consult with legal counsel to ensure their pay data reporting is compliant with both state and federal laws. This includes understanding how SB 1162 interacts with the federal Equal Employment Opportunity Commission's (EEOC) EEO-1 Component 2 data collection, which also requires pay data reporting but has different criteria and deadlines.

## Conclusion

California's SB 1162 represents a significant shift in pay transparency regulations. Employers with operations in California must act quickly to meet the new requirements, ensuring their pay practices are equitable and compliant. Failure to do so could result in costly penalties and reputational damage.
