## DOL's Proposed Rule on Independent Contractor Classification

The U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) announced a proposed rule on July 10, 2026, that aims to redefine the criteria for determining independent contractor status under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA). This proposal could significantly impact how businesses classify workers, affecting industries reliant on freelance and gig workers.

The proposed rule seeks to replace the current interpretation established in 2021, which emphasized an "economic reality" test focusing on two core factors: control over work and opportunity for profit or loss. The new proposal introduces a more comprehensive multi-factor test, aligning closer with the traditional "economic realities" test used prior to 2021. This test will consider six factors, including the worker's opportunity for profit or loss, the permanency of the relationship, the degree of control exercised by the employer, the extent to which the work performed is an integral part of the employer's business, the worker's investment in equipment or materials, and the worker's skill and initiative.

## Implications for Employers

If adopted, this rule will require businesses to reassess their workforce classifications. Misclassification of employees as independent contractors can lead to significant liabilities, including unpaid wages, overtime, and penalties under the FLSA. Industries such as transportation, healthcare, and technology, which heavily rely on independent contractors, may face increased scrutiny and potential reclassification of workers.

Employers should prepare for potential changes by conducting an audit of their current classifications. This involves reviewing contracts and work arrangements to ensure compliance with the new criteria. Employers might also consider consulting with legal counsel to navigate the complexities of the proposed rule and to mitigate risks associated with misclassification.

## Next Steps

The DOL has opened a public comment period until September 8, 2026, inviting stakeholders to submit feedback on the proposed rule. Employers and business groups are encouraged to participate in this process to express concerns or support for specific provisions within the proposal.

The final rule, expected to be issued later this year, will provide more definitive guidance on the classification of independent contractors. Until then, businesses should stay informed of developments and be ready to implement necessary changes to their workforce management practices.

This proposed rule marks another chapter in the ongoing debate over worker classification, balancing the need for labor protections with the flexibility of independent contracting. Employers must remain proactive in adapting to regulatory changes to ensure compliance and avoid costly legal challenges.
