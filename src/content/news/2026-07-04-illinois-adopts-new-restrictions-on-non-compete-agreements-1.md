## Illinois Tightens Non-Compete Agreement Regulations

Illinois Governor signed a new law on June 30, 2026, tightening restrictions on non-compete agreements, effective January 1, 2027. This law, an amendment to the Illinois Freedom to Work Act (820 ILCS 90), impacts employers statewide by setting stricter criteria for enforceability of such agreements.

## Key Provisions

The amendment raises the minimum salary threshold for employees subject to non-compete agreements. Starting in 2027, non-compete clauses are only enforceable for employees earning over $100,000 annually. This marks a significant increase from the previous $75,000 threshold. Additionally, non-solicitation agreements will now require a minimum salary of $60,000, up from $45,000.

The law also mandates that employers provide employees with at least 14 days to review non-compete and non-solicitation agreements. This is designed to offer employees adequate time to consider the terms or seek legal advice. Furthermore, the law requires employers to provide "adequate consideration" for these agreements, which could include financial compensation or additional professional benefits.

## Employer Action Items

Employers in Illinois should immediately review and update their non-compete and non-solicitation agreements to ensure compliance with the new salary thresholds and notice requirements. It is crucial to amend any existing agreements that do not meet these new standards before the law takes effect.

Additionally, HR professionals should audit their current employee agreements to identify those affected by the new law. Employers may need to renegotiate terms with employees who fall below the new salary thresholds or remove non-compete clauses altogether.

Legal counsel should be consulted to ensure that all agreements meet the "adequate consideration" requirement, as failure to comply could render the agreements unenforceable.

## Implications for Employers

This legislative change reflects a growing trend towards limiting the use of non-compete agreements across the United States. Illinois joins other states like California, Washington, and Oregon, which have recently enacted similar restrictions.

Employers should anticipate potential challenges in retaining talent as employees become more mobile. HR departments may need to develop alternative strategies for protecting proprietary information and maintaining competitive advantage, such as enhancing employee retention programs or investing in employee development.

Overall, the new Illinois law represents a significant shift in the regulatory landscape governing employment agreements. Employers must act promptly to align their practices with these changes to avoid legal pitfalls and ensure smooth operations.
